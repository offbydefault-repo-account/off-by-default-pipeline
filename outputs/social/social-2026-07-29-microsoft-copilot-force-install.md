### LinkedIn (~800 characters, max 1,800)

Last fall, Microsoft started automatically installing its AI assistant on millions of Windows 11 business computers. People noticed. There was backlash.

There was also a bug. Copilot surfaced confidential emails — HR conversations, legal documents, financial records — that users hadn't opened and didn't intend to share. Microsoft paused the rollout and said they were listening to feedback.

Then in June, they started again. By July 1st, the forced installation was complete across all eligible Microsoft 365 business users. Same approach. Much less coverage.

The app is an AI assistant that can read your email, summarize your documents, and answer questions by searching across your files, calendar, and Teams conversations. That access is the product, and it's now installed by default on your work computer — without you choosing it.

A few things worth knowing: if you work somewhere with an IT department, your team had a window to opt out before the rollout. Ask whether they did. If you're a solo user or small business on a personal Microsoft 365 subscription, you can uninstall manually — though it may reinstall with future updates.

One geographic footnote that explains everything: Microsoft excluded the entire European Economic Area from this rollout. Same product. Same company. Two different experiences — one based on regulation, one on the absence of it.

### LinkedIn (~800 characters, max 1,800)

There's a version of the Microsoft Copilot story that's about an icon appearing in your Start menu. That version is real, but it's the visible surface of something worth understanding more carefully.

The more telling detail is the European Economic Area exclusion. If you're in the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein, or Norway, Microsoft's forced Copilot installation didn't happen to your computer. You were excluded.

Same company. Same product. Same AI with access to your emails, calendar, and documents. Different outcome — based entirely on which governments are actively enforcing digital consumer protections.

That's not a privacy decision. It's regulatory math. European law makes forced software installation on users' computers expensive. American law, currently, does not. The gap between what US and EU users got from the same company, in the same month, tells you exactly what the company would have chosen if no one was watching.
