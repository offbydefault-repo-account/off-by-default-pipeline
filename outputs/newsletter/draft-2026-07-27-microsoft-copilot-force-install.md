## DRAFT BEGINS

Last fall, a lot of people opened their computers and found an app they didn't install: Microsoft 365 Copilot. It had appeared, courtesy of Microsoft, on millions of Windows 11 business computers. No prompt. No consent screen. It was just there.

There was significant backlash. There was also a bug. The bug let Copilot read and surface confidential emails — HR conversations, legal documents, sensitive financial records — that users hadn't opened and never intended to share. Microsoft paused the rollout.

Then, in June, they started again. The forced installation resumed, and by July 1st, Microsoft had completed the rollout to all eligible Microsoft 365 business users.

The concern wasn't resolved. The press cycle moved on.

---

Here's what Copilot actually is, so you know what was installed.

Microsoft 365 Copilot is an AI assistant built into the Office suite — Word, Excel, Outlook, Teams. When you open a document, it can offer to summarize it. When you're writing an email, it can suggest what to say next. When you ask it a question, it can search across your files, emails, and calendar to find an answer.

That access — across your files, your emails, your calendar — is the product. It's what makes Copilot useful. It's also what made the earlier bug alarming: when Copilot can read all your email and surface "relevant" content, the definition of "relevant" is determined by the AI, not by you. There's a geographic footnote that deserves more attention than it usually gets.

Microsoft excluded users in the European Economic Area from the forced rollout. If you're in the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein, or Norway, the app didn't arrive on your computer uninvited. [VERIFY THIS: confirm EEA exclusion is accurate and complete]

This was not a privacy decision. It was a regulatory one. European digital regulation makes it more expensive to default-install software on users' computers without clear consent pathways. The United States currently has no equivalent framework, which is why American users got the app and European users got a choice.

The same company. The same product. Two entirely different experiences, based purely on which governments were paying attention.

Let's be specific about who this affects and what you can actually do.

If you work at a company that uses Microsoft 365 and has an IT department: the IT team had an opportunity to opt out of the forced installation before it happened, through the Microsoft 365 Apps admin center. Whether they did is worth asking. If the app is already on your computer, they can also uninstall it centrally. Your IT department controls this, not you.

If you're a solo freelancer or small business owner with a personal Microsoft 365 subscription: you don't have the same administrative controls. The app is installed. You can uninstall it manually, though it may reinstall with future updates. [VERIFY THIS: whether manual uninstall is persistent or gets overwritten by Microsoft 365 updates]

To check: open your Start menu on Windows 11, search for "Microsoft 365 Copilot." If it's there, it was installed automatically. You didn't put it there.

There's a counterintuitive thing worth saying here.

Microsoft 365 Copilot showing up as an icon in your Start menu is actually more transparent than most of what's happening in AI right now. You can see it. You can ask what it is. You can decide whether to open it.

The harder version of this story is the AI features that were silently added to existing tools — the ones that now work differently than they did last year, with no announcement, no new icon, no indication that anything changed. Those are everywhere, and they're invisible. The thing that landed as news here landed as news precisely because it was visible. [HUMOR OPPORTUNITY? — FLAG FOR REVIEW]

There's a pattern here that predates AI entirely. A company does something users object to. The company pauses — citing "listening to feedback" — while the news cycle resets. Then the company does the same thing again, more quietly, once fewer people are watching.

What changed between the first rollout and the second wasn't the product or the approach. What changed was the news coverage.
