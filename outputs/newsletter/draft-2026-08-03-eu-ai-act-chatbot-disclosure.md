## Draft

Yesterday, the European Union started enforcing something that sounds like it should already be a rule everywhere.

If you're interacting with a chatbot — a customer service bot, a virtual assistant, an AI agent answering your questions — it now has to tell you it's a chatbot. Not in a buried footnote. At the start of the interaction, clearly. And if you're looking at a deepfake — a video or image of a real person that has been synthetically generated or modified by AI — it now has to be labeled as such.

This is new. It just happened. Yesterday was the first day these rules had teeth.

---

Here's what the rules actually say. Article 50 of the EU AI Act requires any company offering an AI system that people interact with directly — a chatbot, a virtual assistant, an AI customer service agent, an AI therapist — to disclose the AI nature of that system. The disclosure has to happen at the latest at the start of the first interaction. It has to be clear. It cannot be designed to confuse or obscure.

AI-generated or AI-altered images, video, and audio that could be mistaken for authentic recordings of real events or real people must carry visible labels. Deepfakes — synthetic audio or video of a real identifiable person — must carry those labels plus machine-readable markers that other software can detect.

The penalties for non-compliance reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual revenue, whichever is higher. For a company the size of Meta or Google, 3% of worldwide revenue is not a symbolic fine.

The immediate question most readers will have: does this affect me?

If you're in the EU, yes — companies serving you are now legally required to comply. If you're in the US or elsewhere, the honest answer is: probably, eventually, but not because US law requires it.

Here's why. Major AI platforms — the chatbots you use, the tools built into apps you rely on — all have significant EU user bases. For legal and operational reasons, companies generally find it easier to implement the higher-compliance standard globally than to maintain separate product behaviors by region. This is how GDPR ended up improving data practices beyond Europe: not because companies were principled about it, but because maintaining two different systems was expensive and complicated.

That said, companies do manage regional variations when the cost of compliance is high enough and the regulatory downside is limited elsewhere. EU-only features exist. The outcome here isn't guaranteed.

Here's the part most coverage of this story is skipping. The EU transparency rules solve a specific problem: deception. They make it illegal to pretend you're a human when you're not, and illegal to pass off a synthetic video as a real one without labeling it. That's genuinely important. Deception causes real harm.

But the rules don't address consent. Knowing that something is AI-generated is not the same as having been asked whether AI should be used at all. You can be told "I'm an AI" at the start of a conversation and still have had no say in whether your interaction was routed to an AI, whether that interaction will be used to train future models, or whether the company storing that conversation has your permission to do so.

Disclosure is the threshold question — it addresses the most basic form of manipulation. Consent is the harder question — it addresses whether you had any actual agency in the situation. The EU law answers the first. The second question isn't in the law.

The deepfake labeling requirements are actually more technically interesting than the chatbot rules, and they'll matter to anyone who creates or publishes video or audio content.

The chatbot disclosure rules mostly formalize what major platforms were already doing voluntarily in their interfaces. The deepfake rules create new infrastructure requirements — machine-readable provenance markers embedded in synthetic content — that could eventually allow any platform, tool, or person to scan a piece of media and understand whether it was AI-generated before sharing it or acting on it.

The standards for this infrastructure exist (the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity, or C2PA, and Adobe's Content Credentials system are the main ones). Major platforms are in various stages of implementing them. What the EU law does is apply regulatory pressure to build this infrastructure faster. The compliance requirement creates a deadline. Deadlines move industries in ways that good intentions don't.

---

If you create video content — tutorials, vlogs, short-form anything — this is worth paying attention to, even if you're not in the EU and even if you're not creating synthetic content. As platforms implement the provenance infrastructure that deepfake labeling requires, they'll also start marking authentic content as authentic. Being unlabeled in that environment may eventually read as suspicious, or at minimum be treated differently than labeled content.

The EU didn't solve AI. What they did was establish something as a right rather than a courtesy: if you're interacting with an AI, you're entitled to know that. That standard seems basic. It isn't yet universal.

The gap between what's required in Brussels and what's required where you live is, right now, quite wide. Knowing that gap exists changes how you evaluate the chatbots and tools you use every day — and which ones you decide to trust.
