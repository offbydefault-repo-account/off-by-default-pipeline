## SETUP — The problem, plainly stated

Microsoft 365 Copilot is an AI assistant built into Word, Excel, Outlook, and the rest of the Office suite. It can summarize documents, draft emails, answer questions by searching through your files and calendar.

Between October 2025 and March 2026, Microsoft began automatically installing the Copilot app on Windows 11 business computers — computers used by people with Microsoft 365 subscriptions at work.

There was backlash. There was also a bug: Copilot accessed and surfaced confidential emails that users hadn't opened and didn't intend to share. HR conversations. Legal documents. Financial records. The kind of thing you assume stays in your inbox.

Microsoft paused the rollout. They fixed the bug. Then, in June, they started again. By July 1st, the installation was complete across all eligible Microsoft 365 business users.

## EXPLANATION — What Copilot actually does and what access it has

Copilot is not just a chatbot in a sidebar. When fully configured, it has read access to:
- Your emails
- Your calendar
- Your documents stored in Microsoft 365 (OneDrive, SharePoint)
- Your Teams conversations

It uses that access to answer questions, find documents, summarize meetings, and draft responses. That breadth is the product.

The install that just happened puts the app on your computer. It doesn't automatically flip on every access point — those depend on configuration. But the app is there, and for most business users, using it requires very few additional steps. ## NUANCE — What most coverage of this story misses

Coverage focused heavily on the icon appearing on desktops. That's real, but it's the visible part of a bigger pattern.

The more interesting thing: Microsoft excluded users in the European Economic Area from the forced rollout. If you're in the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein, or Norway, this didn't happen to your computer. [VERIFY THIS]

Same product. Same company. Different outcome, based entirely on where you live and which governments are paying attention to what tech companies do to users.

That's not an accident. That's regulatory math. European digital law makes forced software installation expensive. American law, currently, does not.

Second nuance: the icon in your Start menu is actually more honest than most AI deployment right now. You can see it. You can look it up. You can ask what it is.

The harder version of this story is the AI features that were quietly added to existing tools — the Copilot prompts that now appear in Outlook without a new app, the AI suggestions in Word that didn't used to be there. Those changes have no icon. There's nothing to search for in your Start menu. They just happen, and they're happening constantly.

## TAKEAWAY — One clear action

If you work somewhere with an IT department: ask them if they opted out of the Copilot deployment. If they didn't, ask what Copilot's access scope is set to.

If you're on your own — a freelancer, a small business, a solo user with a Microsoft 365 subscription: open Start, search for "Microsoft 365 Copilot," and decide whether you want it. If you don't, uninstall it. Know that it may reinstall with future updates. [VERIFY THIS]

The point isn't that Copilot is dangerous. For some people, it's genuinely useful. The point is that you should decide — not Microsoft — whether it's on your machine and what it has access to.
