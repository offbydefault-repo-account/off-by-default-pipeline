## SETUP — The problem, plainly stated

Meta launched a new AI image generator called Muse Image. It's built into Meta AI — the same assistant that's woven into Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp.

Here's the specific thing that's different about this one: Muse Image lets users tag any public Instagram account and use that account's photos to influence what the AI generates.

Not style influence in a vague way. Likeness influence. The AI looks at your photos to understand what you look like, and then applies that to whatever gets prompted.

If your Instagram account is public, you were automatically included. Not offered the chance to join — enrolled by default.

Private accounts and users under 18 are automatically excluded. Everyone else: opted in, without being asked.

## EXPLANATION — What this actually does

Walk through the mechanism:

- Muse Image is trained on image data (standard for AI image generators)
- It adds a specific feature: public Instagram accounts as "reference accounts"
- A user types a prompt AND tags a public account — the AI generates an image informed by that account's photos
- Result: AI-generated images that can visually resemble the person whose account was tagged [VERIFY THIS: mechanism details — is it style or full likeness?]

What this creates:
- Images of real, identifiable people in scenarios they didn't participate in
- Generated by other users, not by the account holder
- Attached, at least loosely, to their visual identity

SAG-AFTRA immediately told members to opt out. The Creative Artists Agency called the opt-out policy "completely unacceptable." The argument from both: no one's likeness should be used without documented consent.

Meta's argument: these are public images. Public means anyone can see them. This is just... a new use of that.

That argument is technically true and kind of misses the point. [HUMOR OPPORTUNITY? — FLAG FOR REVIEW]

## NUANCE — What most explainers on this miss

Most coverage is framing this as a celebrity problem. SAG-AFTRA. Famous people. Performers.

It's not a celebrity problem.

If you have a public Instagram — even 200 followers, even a small food account, even a hobby page — your photos are in scope. The tool doesn't have a "must be famous" filter. Any public account is a valid reference.

Second nuance: the opt-out exists, but Meta is banking on most people not knowing about it. The feature launched quietly. There was no notification inside Instagram saying "here's a new thing and here's how to opt out of it." You have to know to look.

Third nuance: this story isn't just about images of your face. It's about the shift in what "public" means online. Before Muse Image, posting publicly meant people could see your photos. After Muse Image, it means your photos can be used as inputs to generate new content. Those are not the same thing, and no one asked if that shift was okay.
