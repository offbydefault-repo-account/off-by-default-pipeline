### SETUP

The EU's AI Act has been in development for years. Different parts of it take effect at different times.

Yesterday — August 2nd, 2026 — the transparency obligations kicked in. These are the rules about what companies have to tell you about AI when you're interacting with it.

Two main requirements:

First: any chatbot or AI system you interact with directly has to tell you it's AI. At the start of the interaction. Clearly. Not buried in terms of service.

Second: deepfakes — AI-generated or AI-altered video and audio of real people — have to be labeled as such. Visibly labeled. Plus machine-readable markers embedded in the file that other software can detect.

Penalties for non-compliance: up to €15 million, or 3% of a company's worldwide annual revenue, whichever is higher.

### EXPLANATION — what these rules actually change in practice

Let's be honest about what this does and doesn't do.

The chatbot disclosure rule mostly formalizes what major platforms were already doing voluntarily. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Meta AI — they all identify themselves as AI in their interfaces. The rule matters most for the companies that weren't doing this voluntarily: smaller customer service bots, sales chatbots, AI-powered support systems that were designed to feel as human as possible.

The deepfake rules are more structurally interesting. They require companies to embed provenance markers — machine-readable signals in AI-generated media — that can be detected by other software before you share something or act on it.

The infrastructure for this exists. Adobe's Content Credentials, the C2PA standard — these are the technical frameworks for embedding provenance into images, video, and audio. Some platforms are implementing them. The EU rule creates regulatory pressure to move faster.

What this means practically: in a world where deepfake labeling is enforced, unlabeled media will start to read as suspicious. If you create authentic video content and your content lacks provenance markers, it may be treated differently than content that carries them. Understanding this now — before it becomes a default expectation — is worth your time.

### NUANCE — the thing most explainer videos on this topic get wrong

Here's where the coverage of this story tends to flatten something important.

The EU law addresses deception. It makes it illegal to pretend you're a human when you're not. That matters.

What it doesn't address is consent.

Deception and consent are different problems. Being told you're talking to an AI is meaningfully different from having been asked whether you wanted to interact with an AI at all. You can receive perfect disclosure — "Hi, I'm an AI assistant" — and still have had no say in whether your conversation is being stored, whether it's being used to train future models, or whether the company routing you to an AI instead of a person consulted you about that choice.

Disclosure is the threshold question: am I being lied to? The EU law answers that.

Consent is the harder question: did I agree to this? That question isn't in the law.

### TAKEAWAY — one clear thing to remember or do

When you're evaluating any AI-powered tool, there are now two different questions to ask.

One: does it tell me it's AI? That's the disclosure question. In the EU, it's now legally required. Elsewhere, it's a reasonable thing to expect.

Two: did it ask my permission to be used at all, to store my interactions, to use my data for training? That's the consent question. Almost no company is asking it proactively. But asking it yourself changes how you evaluate what you're agreeing to.

The gap between those two questions is where most of the actual decisions about AI and your life are being made right now — quietly, without your input.
